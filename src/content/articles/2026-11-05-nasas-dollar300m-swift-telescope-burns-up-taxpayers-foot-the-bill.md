# NASA's Swift Telescope Burns Up After 22 Years—Another Costly Mission Ends

NASA's Swift space telescope has re-entered Earth's atmosphere after 22 years studying cosmic explosions, marking the end of a $300 million-plus mission that delivered genuine scientific returns but also exemplifies Washington's appetite for sprawling, long-term spending commitments.

The observatory, launched in 2004, successfully tracked gamma-ray bursts and other high-energy phenomena, contributing real knowledge to astrophysics. By any measure, it was a productive mission—two decades of useful data justifies serious consideration.

But conservatives rightly ask: At what cost, and with what oversight? NASA's budget has ballooned to over $25 billion annually, yet the agency routinely faces cost overruns, schedule delays, and mission creep. The Swift program exemplifies how government agencies lock in decades-long spending with minimal accountability to taxpayers.

Colorado taxpayers foot roughly 10 percent of NASA's bill through federal income taxes. Meanwhile, the state's aerospace and defense sector depends partly on NASA contracts, creating a mixed picture: genuine economic activity offset by wasteful bureaucratic spending.

The real question isn't whether space science matters—it does. It's whether NASA operates with fiscal discipline or simply absorbs whatever Congress appropriates. Watch for the next mega-mission proposal and whether Republicans demand hard cost caps and performance metrics before signing off.
