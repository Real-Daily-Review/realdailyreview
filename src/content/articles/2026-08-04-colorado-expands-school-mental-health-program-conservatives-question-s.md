# Colorado's Youth Mental Health Program Shows Mixed Results as State Expands School Intervention

Colorado is doubling down on placing "near-peer" mental health navigators in schools and community centers—a government-funded initiative that proponents say boosts attendance and behavior, but conservatives worry represents another expansion of state influence over children and parental authority.

According to the Colorado Sun, the program deploys young mental health workers in schools and after-school programs, framed as improving both student outcomes and the professional pipeline. State officials tout improved attendance and behavioral metrics.

Republicans and fiscal conservatives raise legitimate concerns: Does this represent mission creep by government into areas traditionally handled by parents, families, and community institutions? What's the actual cost to Colorado taxpayers, and are there measurable, long-term outcomes—or just feel-good metrics? The program also raises questions about scope: Are these navigators properly credentialed, and what authority do they exercise over minors without parental consent?

The broader issue isn't whether mental health support matters—it does. The question is whether Colorado should expand state-run programs or empower parents and private institutions to address these challenges. Government programs tend to grow, not shrink.

Coloradans should watch whether this initiative expands further into elementary schools and whether the state seeks additional funding without demonstrating genuine, sustained improvement in student outcomes.
