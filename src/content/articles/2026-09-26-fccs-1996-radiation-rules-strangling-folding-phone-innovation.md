# Outdated Radiation Rules Stifle Innovation in Folding Phones

Federal regulators are forcing manufacturers to test cutting-edge folding smartphones using safety standards written nearly three decades ago—a classic case of government bureaucracy choking innovation before it leaves the ground.

The Federal Communications Commission's radiation safety protocols, last substantially updated in 1996, don't account for the geometry and dual-screen architecture of modern foldable devices like Apple's iPhone Duo, Samsung's Galaxy Z Fold, Google's Pixel Fold, and Motorola's Razr+. Manufacturers must navigate outdated testing frameworks designed for flip phones and candy bars, not the sophisticated multidisplay technology consumers are already buying.

According to the *Washington Examiner*, this regulatory lag forces companies to conduct redundant or irrelevant safety assessments, adding cost and delay to product launches without meaningfully protecting consumers. The real-world radiation exposure from these devices remains well within safe limits—but the rulebook hasn't caught up.

This is textbook regulatory capture: Washington bureaucrats move at geological speed while the private sector races ahead. Colorado's tech workers and consumers pay the price through delayed access to innovation and higher device costs.

Conservatives argue the FCC should modernize its standards immediately, relying on actual science rather than 1996 assumptions. The agency should streamline approval processes for technologies that pose no genuine health risk.

Watch whether the FCC updates its radiation standards before the next generation of foldables hits shelves—or whether bureaucratic inertia wins again.
