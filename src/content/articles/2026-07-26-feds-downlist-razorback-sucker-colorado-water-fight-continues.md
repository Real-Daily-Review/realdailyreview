# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker, but Colorado Water Wars Rage On

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status—a move that sounds like progress but masks deeper tensions over Colorado River water management and regulatory overreach.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the call based on population recovery in Arizona rivers, but critics aren't buying it. According to KDVR Fox31 Denver, a national wildlife nonprofit countered that the fish's outlook is "no better today" than when it was first declared endangered. That skepticism matters: the razorback sucker listing has been used to justify strict water allocations and operational constraints on the Colorado River for decades, affecting Colorado farmers, municipalities, and energy producers.

Conservatives argue that endangered species protections, while well-intentioned, often become permanent fixtures that outlive their biological justification—and that bureaucrats are reluctant to admit success and loosen regulatory grip. The downlisting, even to threatened status, keeps federal oversight intact and leaves Colorado water users vulnerable to future restrictions based on shifting scientific claims.

The real question: will this reclassification actually free up water for Colorado's agricultural and municipal needs, or will regulators simply find new reasons to restrict flows? Taxpayers and water-dependent industries should watch whether Interior Department officials use this as cover to maintain the status quo while claiming victory.
