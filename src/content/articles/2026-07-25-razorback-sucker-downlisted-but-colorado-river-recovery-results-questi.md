# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker—But Colorado River Recovery Remains Murky

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status, a move that sounds like progress but masks deeper questions about Colorado River management and the real-world costs of environmental regulation.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the call based on population improvements in Arizona rivers (CPR News). But the reclassification is already drawing fire: a national wildlife nonprofit told KDVR Fox31 Denver that the fish's outlook is "no better today" than when it was first declared endangered—suggesting the decades-long, costly recovery effort may have delivered minimal results.

For Colorado, this matters. The razorback sucker listing has driven expensive water management protocols, habitat restoration projects, and regulatory constraints on river operations that affect agriculture, municipalities, and hydropower. If the fish's actual condition hasn't meaningfully improved despite all that intervention, taxpayers and water users deserve answers about whether the regulatory burden was justified.

Conservatives argue that endangered species protections, while well-intentioned, often impose massive costs with questionable ecological payoff. The razorback case is a textbook example: decades of compliance, millions in spending, and wildlife officials now admitting the fish's real-world survival prospects remain uncertain.

Watch for whether Colorado water managers and agricultural groups push back on any new threatened-species restrictions—and whether state lawmakers demand accountability for the original recovery program's effectiveness.
