# Waymo's Driverless Taxis Hit Denver Streets—But Questions Linger on Safety, Liability, and City Planning

Waymo launched its public driverless taxi service in Denver this week, operating across 60 square miles of the metro area. The move marks a significant test of autonomous vehicle technology in a major U.S. city—and raises hard questions about whether Denver's progressive city government has adequately vetted safety protocols and liability frameworks before letting robots loose on Colorado streets.

The service operates without a steering wheel or pedals, meaning no human can take control if something goes wrong. While tech boosters tout the efficiency gains, conservatives and transportation safety advocates are right to ask: Who pays when a Waymo crashes into a family car? What happens if the system fails in an emergency? Has the city council demanded transparent safety data, or are they simply waving through Silicon Valley's latest experiment?

Denver's permitting process for autonomous vehicles has been notably opaque. The city should require Waymo to post a substantial liability bond, disclose real-time safety metrics to the public, and maintain clear human oversight protocols—not rubber-stamp tech company promises.

Coloradans should watch whether the city demands actual accountability or lets corporate interests write their own rules.
