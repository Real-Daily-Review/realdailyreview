# Democrats' Antisemitism Problem Won't Go Away With Apologies

Abdul El-Sayed's carefully staged apology to Michigan's Democratic Jewish Caucus won't solve the left's deepening credibility crisis on antisemitism. While the Michigan Democrat met with Jewish party members to walk back past remarks, he drew a hard line at distancing himself from Hasan Piker, the streamer facing accusations of antisemitic rhetoric and anti-American extremism.

Republicans and conservative observers say the damage is already done. Mike Rogers, Michigan's Republican nominee, dismissed El-Sayed's mea culpa as a "sham apology" (Fox News). More damning, the Washington Examiner's analysis found that despite media claims Democrats have "distanced themselves" from Piker, only six Democrats have explicitly condemned his latest antisemitic remarks blaming Jews for potential violence against them—a pathetically thin response from a party claiming moral authority on tolerance.

The pattern is clear: Democratic leaders offer performative apologies when caught, then quietly maintain relationships with figures peddling antisemitic conspiracy theories and anti-American extremism. It's the same playbook that's played out nationally as progressive politicians court activist bases hostile to Israel and Jewish Americans.

Colorado Democrats should answer whether they share El-Sayed's refusal to fully break with Piker and similar figures—or whether they'll actually hold their national party accountable for enabling antisemitism.
