# Thousands of Fish Dead at Boyd Lake as Colorado Drought Deepens

Severe drought conditions have killed thousands of fish at Boyd Lake near Loveland, leaving Colorado water managers facing yet another crisis in a state already stretched thin by prolonged dry conditions. Colorado Parks and Wildlife warned residents to exercise caution when handling or harvesting fish from the affected area, citing potential health risks from sick or dead specimens (9News Denver).

The mass die-off underscores a hard reality: Colorado's water crisis isn't theoretical anymore—it's washing up on our shores. While environmentalists often blame development and demand more government restrictions on water use, conservatives point to a simpler culprit: nature. Prolonged drought is a cyclical phenomenon the West has weathered before, and the answer isn't strangling agriculture, energy production, or economic growth with new regulations.

What *is* needed: smart water management that prioritizes Colorado's agricultural heritage and energy independence, not green mandates that sacrifice both for symbolic gestures. The state's farmers and ranchers—who use roughly 80 percent of Colorado's water—shouldn't bear the burden of drought through punitive restrictions while government agencies expand their control over water allocation.

Conservatives should watch whether state officials use this crisis to justify new environmental regulations or land-use controls that ultimately harm Colorado's economy and rural communities.
