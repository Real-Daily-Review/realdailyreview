# Summit County Pumps Brakes on Surveillance Cameras After Public Revolt

Summit County commissioners have temporarily disabled Flock license plate-reading cameras for 30 days, bowing to constituent pressure over warrantless surveillance technology that conservatives say represents exactly the kind of government overreach Coloradans should reject.

The shutdown follows public outcry and commissioner questions about how the cameras were being deployed—a rare win for privacy advocates across the political spectrum (KDVR). Conservatives argue that mass surveillance infrastructure, even when ostensibly aimed at crime prevention, erodes Fourth Amendment protections and creates a chilling effect on lawful activity. The cameras capture and store license plate data on law-abiding citizens without individualized suspicion or judicial oversight.

Meanwhile, Colorado municipalities continue rolling out speed cameras with minimal transparency. Kersey has installed permanent speed cameras on County Road 49, with a 30-day warning period ending Sept. 24 (9News), while similar revenue-generating devices proliferate across the state. Conservatives contend these are de facto tax increases on motorists, not public safety measures—a pattern that should concern Colorado taxpayers already squeezed by inflation and property tax hikes.

The broader question: Will Colorado cities and counties continue building surveillance infrastructure, or will Summit County's 30-day pause signal a genuine reckoning over privacy rights?
