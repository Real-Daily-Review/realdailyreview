# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker, but Colorado River Management Stays Murky

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status, a move that sounds like progress but masks deeper problems with Colorado River management and federal regulatory overreach.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the call despite controversy: a national wildlife non-profit told KDVR that the fish's outlook is "no better today" than when it was first declared endangered. That's a damning indictment of decades of federal intervention and taxpayer-funded recovery programs.

Here's what matters for Colorado: the razorback sucker battle is a proxy war over water rights and river management. Environmental groups use endangered species listings to restrict water use and dam operations, directly affecting Colorado farmers, ranchers, and municipalities dependent on Colorado River allocations. The reclassification doesn't resolve the underlying tension—it just moves the goalpost.

Conservatives argue that federal agencies should either commit to real species recovery with measurable outcomes, or admit the listings are primarily tools for environmental activism. If the fish isn't improving after decades and billions in recovery spending, taxpayers deserve answers about where the money went and why the regulatory burden persists.

Watch whether environmental groups challenge this downlisting in court—a move that would expose whether their real goal is fish recovery or water restriction.
