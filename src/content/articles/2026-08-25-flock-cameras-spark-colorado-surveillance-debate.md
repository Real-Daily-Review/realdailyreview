# San Diego's Flock Camera Push Exposes Surveillance Divide

A man dressed as Darth Vader showed up at San Diego city council to joke about license plate reader cameras—but the real fight over Flock technology isn't funny for Colorado conservatives worried about government surveillance creep.

Flock Safety's automated license plate readers are spreading across American cities, ostensibly to catch criminals. National Review argues law enforcement needs these tools as a "valuable asset" (National Review). But conservatives and privacy advocates see a different threat: mass surveillance infrastructure that collects data on millions of law-abiding citizens' movements without meaningful warrant protections.

Colorado has already seen Flock deployments in several jurisdictions. The technology raises hard questions: Who owns the data? How long is it retained? What prevents mission creep from catching speeders to tracking political opponents or religious gatherings?

Republicans argue that while catching criminals matters, the Fourth Amendment matters more. Warrantless, dragnet surveillance of license plates—even in public—sets a dangerous precedent for government tracking of citizens' daily lives. The Darth Vader stunt may have lightened the mood in San Diego, but it obscures a serious constitutional issue.

Colorado taxpayers and gun owners—already skeptical of government overreach—should watch whether their own cities adopt Flock without robust privacy safeguards and judicial oversight.
