# European Wildfires Trigger Buried WWI, WWII Munitions

Intense wildfires sweeping across Europe this summer are exposing a century-old hazard: thousands of unexploded bombs and mines from World War I and II now detonating as flames consume the landscape.

Explosions have been reported across France, Belgium, Germany, and the Netherlands as extreme heat activates ordnance buried since the 1940s. Firefighters and emergency crews responding to the blazes face an added layer of danger—not just flames, but live munitions erupting without warning beneath scorched earth.

The phenomenon underscores a grim reality: Europe's battlefields remain lethally contaminated. Decades of agricultural and industrial activity have left vast tracts of unexploded ordnance (UXO) dormant but present. Climate-driven wildfires are now the catalyst forcing these weapons to the surface.

For Colorado conservatives watching energy and land-use policy, this serves as a cautionary tale about government land stewardship. While Europe's munitions crisis stems from warfare, poor forest management—often driven by environmental regulations that restrict controlled burns and timber harvesting—has amplified wildfire severity across the American West. Colorado's own forests face similar risks when bureaucratic caution prevents active management.

The real question: Will U.S. policymakers learn that aggressive forest thinning and controlled burns prevent catastrophic fires, or will they double down on restrictions that leave our landscapes as tinderboxes?
