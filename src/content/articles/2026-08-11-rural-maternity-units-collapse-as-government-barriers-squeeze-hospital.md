# Rural Maternity Crisis Deepens as Labor Units Shutter Across America

Nearly 100 labor and delivery units have closed across 35 states in just two years, leaving more than one-third of U.S. counties without adequate maternity care—a trend that threatens rural families and exposes the fragility of America's healthcare infrastructure outside major metros.

The March of Dimes report, covered by 9News Denver and the Daily Caller, documents widening gaps in access as travel times to delivery care stretch longer for expectant mothers in underserved regions. The closures reflect a perfect storm: rural hospital consolidation, Medicaid reimbursement pressures, and regulatory compliance costs that squeeze smaller facilities.

Conservatives point to government-imposed barriers as a culprit. Excessive licensing requirements, Certificate of Needs laws in some states, and inadequate Medicaid reimbursement rates discourage rural hospitals from maintaining maternity units—creating the very scarcity the left then blames on "market failure." The Daily Caller notes these "unnecessary barriers to maternity care" that policy makers have constructed.

For Colorado, rural counties in the northwest and southeast already face significant travel distances to obstetric care. Tightening regulations and Medicaid cuts threaten smaller regional hospitals that serve ranching and agricultural communities.

Conservatives should demand that states eliminate Certificate of Needs restrictions, boost Medicaid reimbursement for rural obstetrics, and cut red tape—not expand government programs that created the problem.
