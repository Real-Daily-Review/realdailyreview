# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker—But Experts Say Colorado River Fish Still Struggling

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status, a move that sounds like progress but masks deeper problems with Colorado River management and federal environmental policy.

The reclassification, announced by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, technically means the species no longer faces imminent extinction. But critics—including national wildlife nonprofits—argue the fish's actual survival prospects haven't improved (KDVR). One conservation group told CPR News the outlook is "no better today" than when the sucker was first listed as endangered decades ago.

Here's the conservative concern: this reclassification exemplifies how federal agencies use regulatory downlistings to declare victory while real problems persist. The razorback sucker's struggles stem from larger Colorado River water management failures—dam operations, water diversions, and competing state claims—that no endangered-species designation can fix. Conservatives argue that sustainable water policy requires balancing environmental goals with Colorado's agricultural and energy needs, not endless regulatory tinkering that ignores root causes.

The downlisting may also reduce protections and oversight, potentially weakening habitat requirements that actually benefit the species. Colorado water users and energy producers should watch whether this move signals the feds are backing away from Colorado River constraints—or simply reshuffling bureaucratic categories while real reform stalls.
