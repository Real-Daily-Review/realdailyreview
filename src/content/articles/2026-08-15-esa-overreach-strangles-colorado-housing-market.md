# Federal Overreach on Endangered Species Is Strangling Colorado's Housing and Land Use

Washington's expansive interpretation of the Endangered Species Act is pricing Coloradans out of their own state—and it's time to rein in the Fish and Wildlife Service.

The FWS and National Marine Fisheries Service have wielded the ESA as a sledgehammer against private landowners and developers, using an overly broad "harm" definition to regulate vast swaths of private property. National Review reports that this authority was never the intent of the original statute. The result: Colorado's already-constrained housing market faces additional regulatory friction, driving up costs and limiting development on land where endangered species *might* exist.

The New York Post documents how the ever-expanding endangered species list has become a de facto tool for restricting affordable housing development nationwide—a problem that hits Colorado's Front Range particularly hard as population pressures collide with environmental restrictions.

Republicans argue the ESA has metastasized beyond species protection into a vehicle for federal land control. The agency's sweeping interpretation of "harm" allows bureaucrats to block projects on private land without clear scientific justification or landowner consent.

Democrats and environmental groups counter that strong protections are necessary to prevent extinction, though they rarely acknowledge the housing and economic costs.

**Watch for:** Congressional efforts to clarify the ESA's scope and rein in agency authority—critical for Colorado's housing affordability crisis.
