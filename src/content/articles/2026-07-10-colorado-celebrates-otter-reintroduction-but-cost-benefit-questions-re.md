# Colorado Marks 50 Years of River Otter Reintroduction—But Questions Linger on Ecological Balance

Colorado is celebrating a half-century since state wildlife officials reintroduced river otters to Colorado waterways in 1974, with Governor Polis hailing the creatures as vital to river health (KDVR Fox31). The reintroduction program has expanded otter populations across the state's rivers and streams.

While the milestone offers a feel-good conservation story, conservatives should ask harder questions about the program's actual costs and ecological tradeoffs. Government-led wildlife reintroduction efforts often lack rigorous cost-benefit analysis and can impose unintended consequences on fishing, ranching, and water management—issues that matter to Colorado's rural economy.

The otter population's growth raises legitimate concerns about predation on native fish species and potential conflicts with agricultural water use along Colorado's increasingly strained river systems. As Colorado faces ongoing water scarcity and competing demands from Front Range growth, taxpayers deserve transparency about whether otter reintroduction aligns with broader water-conservation priorities or simply reflects environmental ideology.

Conservatives should monitor whether state wildlife agencies conduct independent audits of the program's ecological and economic impact—and whether future reintroduction initiatives receive proper legislative oversight rather than bureaucratic discretion.
