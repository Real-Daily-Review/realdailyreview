# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker—But Questions Linger on Colorado River Management

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status, a move that sounds like progress but masks deeper questions about Colorado River management and regulatory overreach.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the call based on population recovery in Arizona rivers, according to CPR News. But the reclassification is already drawing fire: a national wildlife nonprofit told KDVR Fox31 Denver that the fish's outlook is "no better today" than when it was first declared endangered decades ago—raising the obvious question of whether the original listing was justified or simply regulatory mission creep.

For Colorado, this matters. The Colorado River is already stretched thin by interstate compacts, environmental mandates, and competing water demands. Every species listing and recovery program adds another layer of bureaucratic constraint on water allocation and dam operations. Conservatives worry that federal agencies use endangered species designations to expand control over Western water and land use, often with questionable scientific justification.

The downlisting suggests the fish may not have needed the heavy regulatory hand it received. Taxpayers should watch whether this becomes a template for re-examining other Colorado River species protections—or whether the feds simply reclassify and maintain the same restrictions under a new label.
