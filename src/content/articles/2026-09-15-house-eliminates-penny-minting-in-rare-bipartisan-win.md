# House Votes to Ditch the Penny—Finally

The House has passed the Common Cents Act with unanimous support, officially ending penny minting and establishing cash rounding to the nearest nickel for cash transactions. It's a rare moment of bipartisan agreement on a practical fix to a long-standing fiscal irritant.

The penny has been a money-loser for the U.S. Mint for years—it costs more to produce than its face value, a classic example of government inefficiency that conservatives have long criticized. Eliminating it removes a wasteful program and simplifies commerce, particularly for small businesses and retailers who've long complained about the administrative burden of handling pennies.

The rounding mechanism—to the nearest nickel for cash purchases—is straightforward and protects consumers from systematic overcharging. Digital and card transactions remain unaffected, so the change won't disrupt modern payment systems.

This is the kind of unglamorous, common-sense reform that should happen more often in Washington: identify waste, eliminate it, move on. No new regulations, no bureaucratic expansion, just the removal of something that doesn't work.

Conservatives should watch whether the Senate takes up the bill quickly or if it gets bogged down in procedural delays—a test of whether this bipartisan momentum actually translates into action.
