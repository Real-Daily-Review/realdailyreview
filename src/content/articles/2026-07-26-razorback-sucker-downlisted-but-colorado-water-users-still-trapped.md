# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker—But Colorado Water Users Still Face Restrictions

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status, a move that sounds like good news but masks an uncomfortable truth: Colorado water users and farmers will likely remain shackled by the same regulatory constraints that have governed the Colorado River for decades.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the reclassification official, citing recovery efforts in Arizona rivers (CPR News). But critics aren't buying the victory lap. A national wildlife non-profit told KDVR Fox31 Denver that the fish's outlook is "no better today" than when it was first declared endangered—raising the question of why bureaucrats are loosening restrictions at all if the problem persists.

Here's what matters for Colorado: this reclassification won't immediately free up water allocations or reduce the regulatory burden on Western Slope farmers and municipalities already squeezed by Endangered Species Act compliance. The threatened designation still triggers federal oversight, and Colorado's water rights remain entangled in federal litigation and interstate compacts that prioritize environmental mandates over economic reality.

Conservatives argue that decades of ESA enforcement have yielded minimal ecological gains while imposing massive costs on agriculture and energy development. The real test comes next: whether Interior Department officials use this downlisting to actually roll back water restrictions, or simply shuffle the paperwork while keeping Colorado locked in the same regulatory straitjacket.

Watch whether the state's water attorney challenges the threatened designation or pushes for genuine reallocation of Colorado River flows.
