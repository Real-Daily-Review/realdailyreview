# Microfinance Model Crumbles: Another Well-Intentioned Government Scheme Fails to Deliver

Decades of microfinance programs—hailed as a philanthropic silver bullet for lifting the poor out of poverty—are collapsing under the weight of their own flawed assumptions, according to analysis from the *Washington Examiner*.

The model, launched in 1976 by economist Muhammad Yunus, promised that small loans to impoverished women in Bangladesh could spark self-sustaining businesses and repay loans at below-market interest rates. The concept enjoyed bipartisan enthusiasm and billions in funding from governments and foundations worldwide.

But the cracks have been evident for years, critics say. The program's fundamental premise—that poverty could be engineered away through subsidized lending—ignored basic economic reality: not every borrower can generate sufficient returns, and artificially suppressed interest rates distort market signals and mask true risk.

**The Conservative Case:** This represents another cautionary tale of well-meaning government intervention producing disappointing results. When politicians and philanthropists override market mechanisms with subsidies and below-market pricing, they inevitably misallocate capital and create moral hazard. Colorado taxpayers should ask whether state or federal dollars have flowed into similar microfinance ventures locally—and whether those funds could have generated better returns through genuine free-market lending.

Watch for whether Colorado policymakers cite microfinance "success stories" to justify new small-business subsidy programs.
