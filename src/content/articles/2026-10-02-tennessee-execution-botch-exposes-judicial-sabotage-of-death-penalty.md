# Death Penalty Botch Exposes Judicial Sabotage, Not Conservative Principle

Tennessee's botched execution of Christa Pike has reignited debate over capital punishment—but conservatives argue the real scandal isn't the death penalty itself; it's how legislatures and courts have strangled it into dysfunction.

Pike, convicted of a brutal 1994 torture-murder at age 18, was subjected to a failed lethal injection attempt that lasted hours. National Review and the Washington Examiner both point out that this fiasco didn't happen because execution is inherently flawed—it happened because state legislatures and courts have layered the process with procedural obstacles, legal delays, and regulatory complexity that make swift justice nearly impossible.

The Examiner argues bluntly: "State legislators and the courts have turned the death penalty into a grimly farcical process, feeding the cheapest arguments against it." Rather than a referendum on capital punishment's legitimacy, Pike's case indicts the system's deliberate obstruction.

For Colorado, this matters. While Colorado abolished capital punishment in 2020, the broader principle—that government should execute laws cleanly and decisively—applies to every statute on the books. When courts and legislatures deliberately complicate lawful penalties, they undermine public confidence in justice itself.

Conservatives should watch whether states move to streamline execution protocols or whether anti-death-penalty activists use botched procedures as cover to eliminate capital punishment entirely.
