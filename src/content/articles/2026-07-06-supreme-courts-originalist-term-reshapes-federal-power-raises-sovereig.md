# Supreme Court's Originalist Term Reshapes Power—But Sovereignty Concerns Remain

The Supreme Court's latest term delivered a landmark victory for originalist jurisprudence, fundamentally rebalancing power between Congress, the executive branch, and the states in ways that will ripple through Colorado policy for years to come.

The centerpiece: *Trump v. Slaughter*, which dismantled the 90-year-old framework allowing Congress to shield heads of "independent" agencies from presidential accountability. Conservatives argue this restores constitutional separation of powers and checks runaway bureaucratic overreach—a win for limited government that could constrain federal regulators' grip on Colorado's energy, water, and land-use decisions.

But the term also sparked sharp debate over national sovereignty. The Washington Examiner's editorial board warned that certain rulings effectively handed Beijing a strategic victory by weakening America's ability to govern its own borders and citizenship—a sovereignty concern that transcends typical left-right splits and cuts to the nation's self-preservation.

For Colorado conservatives, the originalist shift promises relief from federal regulatory expansion. Expect challenges to EPA overreach on water and air rules, and renewed state authority over energy development and public lands management.

Watch whether Colorado's Republican-led legislature moves to capitalize on reduced federal agency power—and whether the state's progressive cities attempt to circumvent the Court's originalist turn through state-level regulation.
