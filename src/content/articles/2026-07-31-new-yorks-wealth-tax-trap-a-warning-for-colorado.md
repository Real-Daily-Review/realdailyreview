# New York's Wealth Tax Creep Shows Why Colorado Must Guard Against Progressive Overreach

New York's experiment in soaking the rich has become a cautionary tale for Colorado taxpayers watching their own state drift left.

Governor Kathy Hochul promised to tax only the wealthiest 1%—luxury second-home owners in Manhattan. But within months, the tax base exploded. New York estimated 10,000 properties in May. By July, the list had swelled to 30,000 and beyond, according to the *Washington Examiner*. The definition of "luxury" kept expanding, pulling in middle-class property owners who thought they were safe.

This is how progressive taxation works in practice: politicians set a narrow target, then bureaucrats widen the net. The criteria—acreage, livestock, hired hands—became tools for government clerks to pull ordinary Coloradans into the crosshairs.

Colorado's Democrat-controlled legislature has already floated similar wealth and property tax schemes. Republicans argue these taxes inevitably creep downward, hitting small business owners, ranchers, and retirees who built assets over decades. Hochul's bait-and-switch proves the pattern: define "the rich" narrowly, then redefine it when revenue falls short.

Coloradans should watch closely whether state Democrats propose expanded property or wealth taxes in the 2025 session—and demand ironclad limits on how those definitions can change.
