# Feds Downlist Razorback Sucker—But Colorado Water Wars Aren't Over

Federal wildlife officials have downlisted the razorback sucker from endangered to threatened status, a move that sounds like progress but masks deeper tensions over Colorado River water management and regulatory overreach.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the call based on population recovery in Arizona rivers, but the reclassification is already drawing fire. According to KDVR Fox31 Denver, a national wildlife nonprofit argues the fish's outlook is "no better today" than when it was first declared endangered—raising questions about whether the downlisting reflects genuine recovery or regulatory theater.

Here's what matters for Colorado: the razorback sucker saga exemplifies how endangered species protections have constrained water use across the Colorado River Basin for decades. Conservatives argue that environmental mandates, however well-intentioned, have prioritized a single fish species over the water needs of farmers, municipalities, and energy producers across the West. The threatened status may ease some restrictions, but don't expect immediate relief for Colorado water users.

The downlisting also highlights the arbitrary nature of federal wildlife classifications—a reminder that regulatory decisions often rest on shifting science and political pressure rather than clear thresholds for success.

Watch whether this reclassification actually translates to loosened water restrictions for Colorado agriculture and municipalities, or whether environmental groups successfully challenge the decision in court.
