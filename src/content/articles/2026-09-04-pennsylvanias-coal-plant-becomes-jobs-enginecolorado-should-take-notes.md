# Coal Plant Closure Becomes Blueprint for Energy Transition—Without Killing Jobs

Pennsylvania's largest coal-fired power plant didn't become a graveyard—it became a launchpad. Eighteen months after the smokestacks fell in Homer City, Indiana County, 1,800 local tradespeople are now building what developers call an "energy and data innovation campus" on the same site, according to the *Washington Examiner*.

The project offers a counternarrative to the left's energy transition playbook: economic revitalization without abandoning the workers who powered the old economy. Rather than leaving coal communities to decay while federal subsidies chase solar and wind elsewhere, this model retains skilled labor, maintains tax revenue, and leverages existing infrastructure.

For Colorado, the lesson cuts deep. The state's coal-dependent communities in northwest Colorado and the San Juan Basin face similar crossroads as the Biden administration accelerates coal plant retirements. Conservatives argue that Colorado should study Pennsylvania's approach—partnering with private developers to repurpose retired plants for data centers, manufacturing, or next-generation energy production—rather than relying on government retraining programs and welfare-style transition funds that often fail displaced workers.

The real test: whether Colorado's Democratic-controlled legislature will embrace market-driven solutions or double down on top-down mandates that leave energy workers behind.

Watch whether Colorado's coal communities demand similar private-sector partnerships before accepting further plant closures.
