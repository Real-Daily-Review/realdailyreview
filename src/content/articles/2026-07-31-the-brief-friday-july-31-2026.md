# Real Daily Review — The Brief
### Friday, July 31, 2026

Colorado conservatives head into the weekend watching a Jefferson County school board flirt with another tax hike, a national debate over federal land and mineral rights that hits home hard in the West, and a Republican Senate standoff that could shape the next attorney general of the United States.

---

## JeffCo Schools Eye Tax Hike Amid Deep Voter Distrust

Jefferson County public school board members are floating a tax measure to address budget pressures they say stem from declining enrollment — but voters aren't buying it. Complete Colorado reports that distrust is already clouding the conversation before any measure reaches the ballot. Colorado taxpayers have seen this playbook before: enrollment drops, administrators protect bureaucracy, and the bill gets handed to homeowners. JeffCo voters would be wise to demand a hard look at spending cuts before anyone talks about reaching deeper into their pockets.

---

## Federal Land, Federal Blindness — and Colorado Pays the Price

The federal government owns 28% of America's land and, according to reporting this week, has little idea what mineral resources lie beneath it. President Trump ordered the Pentagon to map vulnerable supply chains and push toward domestic sourcing — a sound instinct. But the Interior Department's own data gaps underscore a decades-long failure of federal land management. For Colorado, where the feds control vast stretches of the Western Slope, this isn't abstract: energy workers, ranchers, and mining communities have long paid the price for Washington's bureaucratic indifference to what lies beneath their feet.

## Separating Colorado Government From Party Politics

A commentary from Complete Colorado makes the case that Colorado government has no business granting special recognition or privileges to political parties — and calls the move to separate state from party a "courageous" one. Conservatives should agree: government entanglement with party structures invites favoritism and insider dealing, regardless of which party benefits. Limited government means limited government involvement in political machinery. Colorado lawmakers who want to earn back public trust should take this argument seriously.

---

## GOP Senate Standoff Over AG Nominee Heats Up

Sen. Thom Tillis is holding firm against President Trump's threat to pull Todd Blanche's attorney general nomination, insisting the White House must honor a prior commitment. The standoff is drawing attention across the party. Republicans argue the Senate's advice-and-consent role is not a rubber stamp, and that intra-party negotiations require good faith on both sides. How this resolves will matter for conservatives watching whether the next AG can be confirmed with the votes and credibility needed to take on the job.

---

## Seattle's Police Crisis Is a Warning for Colorado's Blue Cities

Seattle's police chief has resigned following a widely criticized emergency response to a shooting earlier this week. Mayor Katie Wilson announced the departure of Shon Barnes after the response drew sharp public backlash. Conservatives have said for years that progressive city governments undermine law enforcement and then act surprised when things go wrong. Denver and Boulder should be paying attention: the soft-on-crime, defund-the-police ideology that gutted Seattle's department doesn't stop at the state line.

---

## What to Watch

Keep your eye on the Jefferson County school board — if board members move forward with a tax measure, Colorado conservatives need to show up early and loudly before it reaches the ballot. On the federal land front, watch whether the Trump administration's mineral-mapping push translates into real action that opens Western Slope resources to responsible development, or stalls in the same bureaucratic mud that's always slowed it down. And the Tillis-Trump AG standoff bears watching: a botched confirmation process could leave a critical law enforcement post in limbo at exactly the wrong moment.
