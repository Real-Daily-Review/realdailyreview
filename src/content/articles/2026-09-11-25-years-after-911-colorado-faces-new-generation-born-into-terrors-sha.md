# A Generation Born After 9/11 Now Faces a World Still Shaped by Terror

A quarter-century after the 2001 attacks, Colorado students who never experienced that day firsthand are learning what shaped American foreign policy, national security, and the War on Terror (CPR News). The lessons are stark: younger generations grapple with a national trauma that has become history to them, even as its consequences remain live policy.

National Review warns that despite two decades of counterterrorism efforts, "Sharia supremacism is on the march, still"—a reminder that ideological threats persist regardless of military interventions or diplomatic overtures. Just The News notes that every president since 2001 has been defined by 9/11's aftermath, from the War on Terror through ongoing Middle East conflicts and tensions with Iran.

For Colorado, this matters. The state hosts military installations, defense contractors, and intelligence operations tied directly to post-9/11 security architecture. Younger voters and workers here will inherit both the strategic commitments and the fiscal costs of three decades of counterterrorism spending.

Conservatives argue that national security cannot be treated as yesterday's news—vigilance against ideological extremism and state sponsors of terror remains essential, even as public attention wanes. The question for Colorado taxpayers: as threats evolve, are we still investing wisely in defense, or are we complacent?
