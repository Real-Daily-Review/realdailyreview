# A Quarter-Century After 9/11: America's Memory Fades While Threats Remain

A generation of Colorado students now grapples with 9/11 as history rather than lived trauma—a troubling sign that national resolve against terrorism may be weakening at precisely the wrong moment.

Twenty-five years after the attacks, the lessons are fragmenting. CPR News reports that students born after 2001 are encountering the tragedy through education programs rather than collective memory. That institutional approach has merit, but conservatives worry it sanitizes the stakes: the ideological war that defined the post-9/11 era remains unfinished.

National Review argues bluntly that "Sharia supremacism is on the march, still," warning that complacency about radical Islam threatens to undo hard-won security gains. Just The News notes that every president since 2001—from Bush through Biden—has shaped foreign policy around counterterrorism, yet the coherence of that strategy has eroded.

For Colorado, this matters directly. The state hosts military installations, defense contractors, and intelligence operations tied to counterterrorism. A generation losing institutional memory of why those investments exist risks political pressure to defund or redirect them toward progressive priorities.

Republicans argue that memorial education must emphasize not nostalgia but vigilance—the threats that motivated post-9/11 security doctrine haven't disappeared; they've evolved.

Watch whether Colorado's schools teach 9/11 as a cautionary tale about the cost of unpreparedness, or as a historical artifact stripped of its strategic urgency.
