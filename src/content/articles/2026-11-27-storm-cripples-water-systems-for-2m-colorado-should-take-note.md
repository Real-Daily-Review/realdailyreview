# Eastern Seaboard Water Crisis Exposes Infrastructure Fragility as Storms Knock Out Filtration Plants

Thanksgiving storms that battered the East Coast exposed a hard truth: America's aging water infrastructure is one power outage away from crisis. Nearly 2 million residents in Maryland's Prince George's and Montgomery counties were ordered to conserve water on Thursday after storms disabled two filtration plants serving the Washington, D.C. suburbs, forcing WSSC Water to issue an "Essential Water Use Only" directive (Washington Examiner).

The incident underscores what conservatives have long warned: decades of deferred maintenance and underinvestment in critical infrastructure—compounded by regulatory red tape that slows repairs and upgrades—leave communities vulnerable. While the Left often demands massive federal spending on vague "infrastructure" initiatives, the real problem is government's failure to maintain the basics.

Colorado faces similar vulnerabilities. The state's water systems depend on aging infrastructure built decades ago, and climate variability means reliability cannot be taken for granted. As Colorado's population grows and water demands intensify, policymakers must prioritize maintenance and modernization over new regulations that drive up costs without improving service.

Conservatives argue that fixing infrastructure requires cutting bureaucratic delays, not expanding government programs. The question for Colorado taxpayers: Will state and local leaders focus on core services, or will they use crises like this to justify tax hikes and regulatory expansion?

Watch whether Colorado's water authorities use this Maryland incident to demand emergency funding—and whether they'll commit to efficiency reforms first.
