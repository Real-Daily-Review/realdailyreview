# Real Daily Review — The Brief
### Friday, November 6, 2026

The midterm results are still being digested in Washington and across the country, but Colorado conservatives have plenty to watch — from a national security fight in Congress to a violent night on the Front Range. Here's what you need to know this morning.

## Hegseth Digs In as Democrats Push Impeachment

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth went on offense this week, posting a defiant video message urging Congress to approve $350 billion in military funding while vowing to fight Democratic impeachment efforts head-on. Republicans argue Hegseth is doing exactly what the Pentagon needs — cutting bureaucratic fat and rebuilding warfighting capability — and that Democrats are using impeachment as a political weapon rather than a legitimate oversight tool. Colorado's defense contractors and military families at bases like Fort Carson and Buckley Space Force Base have a direct stake in whether that $350 billion gets approved.

## Loveland Shooting, I-25 Crash Leave Two Seriously Injured

Loveland police arrested three suspects — including a minor — following an overnight shooting that triggered a crash near Interstate 25 and left two people seriously injured. Front Range communities have watched violent crime creep northward from Denver for years, and incidents like this are a reminder that soft-on-crime policies have consequences well beyond city limits. Colorado conservatives will be watching whether prosecutors pursue charges that match the severity of what happened on that highway.

## Denver Cleans Up Its Public Safety Hiring — After Getting Caught

Denver's Civil Service Commission has implemented most of an auditor's recommendations to improve hiring practices for police and firefighters, including better software usage and enhanced security measures. Credit where it's due — but the fact that it took a formal audit to get the city to run a competent hiring process for its most essential employees tells you everything about how Denver's progressive city government operates. Taxpayers shouldn't need an auditor to force basic accountability.

## The Midterm Reckoning: What Went Wrong for the GOP

Two themes are dominating conservative post-mortems this morning: Republicans argue that treating congressional allies with contempt cost the party at the ballot box, and that a political movement built around a single man will eventually lose its ability to elect anyone else. One analysis puts it plainly — a party that acts as if it is only one man will lose its ability to get anybody else elected. Colorado Republicans, who have struggled to hold statewide offices for years, should be paying close attention to this conversation. Building a durable bench matters.

## New Jersey Mayor Who Charged at Constituent Gets Voted Out

Pemberton Township Mayor Matthew Bianchini, who made national headlines after charging at a resident during a council meeting last month, was decisively defeated in his mayoral race. The story is a useful reminder that voters — when given a clear choice — still hold elected officials accountable for basic conduct. It's a lesson that applies at every level of government, from Trenton to Denver.
