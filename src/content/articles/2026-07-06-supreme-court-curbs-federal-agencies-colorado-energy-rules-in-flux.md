# Supreme Court's Originalist Win Could Reshape Federal Power—and Colorado's Regulatory Landscape

The Supreme Court's latest term delivered a landmark victory for constitutional originalists, fundamentally constraining federal agency power in ways that will ripple through Colorado's energy, water, and land-use regulations for years (The Daily Wire).

The centerpiece: *Trump v. Slaughter*, which stripped Congress's ability to shield heads of "independent" agencies from presidential removal. This 90-year-old doctrine—a cornerstone of the administrative state—is now gone. Conservatives argue this restores constitutional accountability and checks the unelected bureaucracy that has strangled Colorado's oil, gas, and coal industries with rules written in Washington without local input.

Republicans say the decision returns power to elected officials answerable to voters, not faceless regulators. The left counters that dismantling agency independence threatens environmental protections and worker safety standards.

For Colorado specifically, this matters enormously. Federal agencies control vast swaths of state land and water policy. Weakening their insulation from presidential oversight could accelerate energy development approvals and streamline permitting—or, under a Democratic administration, tighten environmental enforcement. Either way, Colorado's economy, water rights, and energy sector face new uncertainty.
