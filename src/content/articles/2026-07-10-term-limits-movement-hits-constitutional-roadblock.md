# Congressional Term Limits Hit Constitutional Wall—Again

Fourteen state legislatures, frustrated with career politicians in Washington, have passed resolutions demanding congressional term limits through an Article V constitutional convention. But they're running into a hard constitutional fact: the Supreme Court has already ruled twice that Congress cannot impose term limits on itself, and the states lack the power to do it unilaterally through legislation.

Conservatives have long championed term limits as a check on entrenched incumbency and government bloat. Republicans argue that limiting House members to six years and senators to two six-year terms would reduce the influence of special interests, cut wasteful spending, and force genuine accountability to voters rather than lobbyists. The movement reflects deep skepticism of Washington's permanent political class.

The constitutional obstacle is real. The Supreme Court determined in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton* (1995) that states cannot unilaterally impose term limits on federal representatives—only a constitutional amendment can. That means the Article V route is the only legal path forward, requiring 34 states to call a convention and 38 to ratify any amendment.

For Colorado conservatives, this underscores a broader frustration: Washington's structural incentives favor career politicians over reform. Twenty more states would need to join the effort to trigger a convention.

Watch whether Colorado's legislature joins the push—and whether the movement gains momentum or stalls short of the 34-state threshold.
