# NASA's Swift Telescope Burns Up After 22 Years—Another Reminder of Space Debris Risk

NASA's Swift space telescope completed its final mission Wednesday when it plunged through Earth's atmosphere after 22 years studying cosmic explosions. The satellite, which investigated gamma-ray bursts and other high-energy phenomena, re-entered safely without incident, according to reports from 9News Denver and the New York Post.

While the controlled re-entry avoided populated areas, the event underscores a growing concern for space policy: orbital debris management and the long-term costs of maintaining aging satellites. Conservatives have long questioned whether NASA's sprawling budget—currently exceeding $25 billion annually—delivers sufficient return on investment for taxpayers, particularly when missions reach their end-of-life phase.

The Swift Observatory's two-decade run produced valuable scientific data, but questions linger about prioritization. Republicans and fiscal hawks argue that NASA should focus resources on missions with direct national security or economic benefits—such as lunar exploration supporting American dominance in space—rather than spreading funding across numerous research initiatives.

The space agency's aging infrastructure and decommissioning challenges highlight inefficiencies that conservative critics say plague federal science spending. As space becomes increasingly contested terrain between the U.S., China, and private industry, Colorado taxpayers should watch whether Congress demands greater accountability for how NASA allocates the billions it receives annually.
