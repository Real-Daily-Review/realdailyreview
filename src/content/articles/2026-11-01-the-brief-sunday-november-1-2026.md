## Avian Flu Hits Another Colorado Dairy

State health officials confirmed avian flu has been detected at another Colorado dairy operation, marking another blow to the state's agricultural sector. Officials are telling the public that pasteurized milk remains safe — which is reassuring, but ranchers and dairy producers are the ones absorbing the operational pain. Colorado's ag industry deserves straight answers from state government about containment, compensation, and what's being done to protect producers who are already squeezed by input costs and regulatory burdens.

## MAGA Has the Base — But Does It Have the Math?

Washington Examiner chief political correspondent Byron York lays out a sobering reality for Republicans heading into Tuesday: President Trump commands a 95% approval rating among self-identified Republicans, but the party's internal mechanics may not be enough to overcome a difficult electoral environment. Enthusiasm among the base is not the same as winning margins in swing districts. Colorado Republicans — fighting uphill battles in suburban Denver and the Front Range — should take note: base loyalty wins primaries; persuasion wins general elections.

## Democrats Eye Election-Law Overhauls in Battleground States

Republicans are bracing for what polls suggest will be a strong Democratic night on Tuesday, and the consequences could extend well beyond this cycle. A blue wave in key battleground states could open the door to major election-law rewrites — the kind of structural changes that reshape how the 2028 presidential election gets decided. Democrats have long pushed for expansions to mail-in voting, same-day registration, and ballot harvesting. Conservatives say these changes erode election integrity. Colorado voters, already living under some of the most permissive election laws in the country, should watch this closely.

## The Ilhan Omar Question — And Why It Matters

President Trump has called on Rep. Ilhan Omar to resign from Congress over alleged immigration fraud, but the Constitution is clear: only the House of Representatives has the power to remove one of its own members. That's not a technicality — it's a feature of the separation of powers. The real question conservatives should be asking is whether House Republicans have the spine to pursue the matter through proper constitutional channels, or whether this becomes another case of loud rhetoric and no follow-through.
