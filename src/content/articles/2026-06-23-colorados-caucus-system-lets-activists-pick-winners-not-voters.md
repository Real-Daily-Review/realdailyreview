# Colorado's Primary Problem: When Party Activists, Not Voters, Pick Winners

Colorado's caucus-and-assembly system is narrowing the field of viable candidates to those who satisfy ideological activists—not the broader electorate that will actually vote in November.

According to Complete Colorado, only two of 18 Republican gubernatorial candidates advanced out of the state assembly process, illustrating how the current primary structure filters candidates through a gauntlet of party faithful rather than primary voters. The system rewards candidates who energize the base, often at the expense of general-election appeal.

Kent Thiry, a prominent Colorado voice, has warned of a "tyranny of the minority" in Colorado elections, arguing that ideological factions dominate party primaries and shape outcomes unrepresentative of most voters (Complete Colorado). Meanwhile, unaffiliated voters—a growing bloc in Colorado—face their own constraints: party-registered voters who skip caucuses are "simply limiting your options under the current rules," as Complete Colorado notes.

**The conservative case:** Party activists should have a voice, but the current system risks nominating candidates too ideologically pure to win statewide in an increasingly purple Colorado. Broader primary participation—or primary reform—could produce nominees with stronger general-election viability.

**What to watch:** Whether Colorado Republicans and Democrats reform their nomination processes before 2028, or whether unaffiliated voter growth continues to reshape state politics outside party structures.
