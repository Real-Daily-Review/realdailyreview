# Supreme Court's Emergency Docket Becomes New Battleground Over Judicial Speed vs. Due Process

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson is sounding the alarm about the Supreme Court's emergency docket, warning it grants certain litigants a "fast pass" that bypasses standard appellate review—a complaint that reveals deep fractures over how quickly the high court should act on contested cases.

The controversy centers on what critics call the "shadow docket." During President Trump's second term, the Justice Department has repeatedly used emergency filings to ask the Court to stay lower-court rulings blocking administration policies (New York Post). Jackson argues this process undermines judicial deliberation and favors those with resources to exploit it.

Conservatives counter that the emergency docket serves a vital function. National Review highlighted a case where a state court ordered an Orthodox Jew to demand a rabbinical court reverse a religious ruling—precisely the kind of religious-liberty overreach the emergency docket can swiftly correct. Republicans argue the docket protects constitutional rights when lower courts act unlawfully and delays would cause irreparable harm.

The dispute reflects a broader conservative-versus-progressive divide: Does expedited review protect liberty, or does it short-circuit careful jurisprudence? Colorado gun owners, energy developers, and religious organizations should watch whether the Court continues using emergency filings to block state regulations—or whether Jackson's critique gains traction among swing justices.
