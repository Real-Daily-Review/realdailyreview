# Colorado Election System Faces Scrutiny as Griswold's Plurality Victory Reignites Reform Debate

Colorado's winner-take-all primary system is broken—and Secretary of State Jena Griswold's recent victory with a fractured field proves it. According to Complete Colorado, a candidate can win nomination with a small minority of voters even when another candidate is more broadly preferred, a structural flaw conservatives have long flagged as undemocratic.

Republicans argue the current system allows ideologically extreme candidates to capture nominations without genuine majority support, distorting Colorado's political direction. The plurality-wins model, they contend, fails to reflect the actual preferences of the broader electorate and undermines the legitimacy of general election matchups.

Defenders of the status quo—primarily Democrats and election administrators—have resisted reform efforts, citing implementation costs and operational complexity. The Denver Post Editorial Board's recent endorsement cycle reflects the establishment's comfort with existing rules.

For Colorado taxpayers and conservatives, the stakes are clear: a broken primary system can elevate candidates hostile to fiscal restraint, Second Amendment rights, and energy development. Without ranked-choice voting or runoff mechanisms, Colorado Republicans risk nomination battles that don't reflect party strength heading into November.

Watch for whether the GOP-controlled legislature acts on election reform in the 2027 session—or whether Griswold's plurality victory becomes the new normal.
