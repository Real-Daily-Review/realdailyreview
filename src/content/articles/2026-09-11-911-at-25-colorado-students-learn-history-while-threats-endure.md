# Twenty-Five Years After 9/11: A Generation Grapples with History While Threats Persist

Colorado students born after 2001 are learning about 9/11 as history, not lived trauma—a generational shift that raises hard questions about institutional memory and vigilance.

According to CPR News, younger Coloradans who never experienced the attacks firsthand are now studying counterterrorism and national security through educational frameworks that treat September 11th as a historical event rather than a defining national moment. This distance matters: without direct memory of the attacks' scale and consequences, there's risk that complacency replaces the resolve that once drove security policy.

National Review warns that the broader threat landscape hasn't disappeared—"Sharia supremacism is on the march, still." The Washington Examiner notes that a 2006 plot to detonate transatlantic flights from London was thwarted only through intelligence work and international cooperation, a reminder that vigilance prevented catastrophe just five years after 9/11.

For Colorado, this historical reckoning intersects with current security debates: airport screening protocols, border security, and counterterrorism funding remain live policy questions. Conservatives argue that weakening post-9/11 security measures or treating terrorism as a historical curiosity rather than an ongoing threat invites complacency.
