# Colorado's Primary Problem: When Party Activists, Not Voters, Pick Winners

Colorado's primary system is increasingly dominated by party activists and ideological factions, leaving rank-and-file voters—especially unaffiliated Coloradans—on the sidelines.

According to Complete Colorado, party-registered voters who skip caucuses are "simply limiting their options under the current rules." The problem runs deeper: Kent Thiry has warned of a "tyranny of the minority" in Colorado elections, where ideological factions control party primaries and shape outcomes that don't reflect most voters' preferences.

This matters for Colorado conservatives and taxpayers. When primary voters skew ideological, nominees often reflect activist priorities rather than mainstream concerns—fiscal discipline, limited regulation, and practical governance. Unaffiliated voters, now a significant bloc in Colorado, have little voice in determining which candidates reach the general election.

Republicans and conservatives argue the caucus-and-primary structure empowers party insiders over ordinary citizens. The result: nominees picked by the most engaged (and often most ideological) voters, not the broader electorate. This can produce candidates who struggle in general elections or govern from the ideological margins rather than the center.

Watch whether Colorado Republicans and Democrats reform their primary processes to give unaffiliated voters a real seat at the table—or whether activist control of nominations continues to narrow Colorado's political choices.
