# Twenty-Five Years On: Colorado Schools Grapple With 9/11 as History, Not Trauma

A quarter-century after the September 11 attacks, Colorado educators face a generational divide: teaching students born well after 2001 about a day that shaped national security policy but feels increasingly distant to younger Americans.

According to CPR News, Denver-area schools are incorporating 9/11 into curricula as historical event rather than lived national trauma. Students encounter the attacks through classroom lessons and learning labs rather than collective memory, raising questions about how to preserve the lessons of that day without the emotional weight that defined the immediate aftermath.

National Review argues the broader strategic picture remains unsettled. While the acute terror threat has diminished, conservatives warn that ideological threats—including what the outlet describes as "Sharia supremacism"—continue to advance globally, even as American attention has shifted.

For Colorado, the shift reflects a practical reality: the state's schools now teach 9/11 much as they teach Pearl Harbor—as pivotal history requiring context and understanding, but no longer as a defining national emergency. The challenge for educators is ensuring that younger Coloradans grasp both the historical significance and the enduring security lessons without sensationalizing the tragedy.

Watch whether Colorado schools adopt curricula that balance historical accuracy with age-appropriate counterterrorism education, or whether the day becomes merely another date in the textbook.
