### Tuesday, November 17, 2026

Colorado conservatives are waking up to a week defined by federal dysfunction, economic jitters, and a state government that still hasn't figured out that chasing capital away isn't a development strategy.

## Ground Stop Fallout Keeps Hitting Travelers

Last week's nationwide FAA ground stop is still rippling through airport schedules, with passengers reporting disruptions to upcoming travel plans. For Coloradans flying in and out of DIA — one of the busiest hubs in the country — this is a real-world reminder of what happens when federal agencies responsible for critical infrastructure can't keep the lights on. Republicans have long argued that bloated federal bureaucracies prioritize everything except core competency. This is what that looks like at 30,000 feet.

## Bank System Outages Rattle Customers

Customers at some of the nation's largest banks reported being locked out of their online accounts Monday, raising fresh questions about the resilience of the financial system. Whether this was a technical failure or something more systemic, Colorado families and small business owners who depend on reliable access to their money deserve answers — not reassurances from regulators who were caught flat-footed. Conservatives say the lesson is simple: the more centralized and federally entangled the banking system becomes, the more fragile it gets.

## Colorado's Data Center Moratorium Is a Capital Repellent

Complete Colorado is out with a sharp piece on what the push to impose moratoriums on data center development in Colorado actually accomplishes: it tells capital to go somewhere else — and capital listens. Colorado's energy grid and land-use debates are legitimate policy conversations, but conservatives argue that reflexive regulatory bans are a blunt instrument that kills jobs, tax base, and economic momentum. If Colorado Democrats want to wonder why businesses keep eyeing Texas and Arizona, they need look no further than their own regulatory instincts.

## Adams County Weighs Housing Density Overhaul

Adams County commissioners are taking up a housing density plan that would allow more duplexes, townhomes, and accessory dwelling units in urban areas, along with reduced minimum setbacks. Republicans and property-rights advocates will want to watch this closely — there's a meaningful difference between reducing government-imposed zoning barriers that block private development and using density mandates to override local community preferences from above. The devil, as always, is in the details of who's driving the bus.

## Trump Takes Divided Cabinet to China for High-Stakes Talks

President Trump is heading to Asia for a high-stakes meeting with Xi Jinping, bringing both Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick — two Cabinet heavyweights Trump himself acknowledges hold opposing views on China trade policy. For Colorado's agricultural producers, energy exporters, and manufacturers who live and die by trade conditions, the outcome of these talks matters enormously. Conservatives will be watching to see whether the administration holds firm on protecting American economic interests or blinks under pressure from Wall Street's globalist wing.

## What to Watch

Keep your eyes on the Adams County housing density vote — it's a bellwether for how far Colorado's local governments will go in trading community control for top-down planning mandates.
