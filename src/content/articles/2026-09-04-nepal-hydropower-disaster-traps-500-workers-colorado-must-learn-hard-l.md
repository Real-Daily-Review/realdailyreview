# Nepal Hydropower Disaster: A Cautionary Tale for Colorado's Energy Future

A glacier collapse in Nepal has left roughly 500 workers missing or trapped in hydropower tunnels—a stark reminder of the risks inherent in aggressive hydroelectric development, particularly in mountainous terrain vulnerable to climate volatility.

Rescuers pulled two workers alive from the Trishuli 3A tunnel nine days after catastrophic flooding swept through the region (Washington Examiner, New York Post). But the scale of the disaster is staggering: approximately 900 workers are unaccounted for across 12 hydropower projects, with over 500 believed trapped underground (9News Denver).

**The Colorado angle matters here.** While Colorado's hydroelectric infrastructure is far more developed and regulated than Nepal's, the state remains dependent on mountain water systems and glacier-fed rivers for power generation and irrigation. Conservatives and energy advocates argue that hydropower remains a reliable, carbon-free energy source—but this Nepal catastrophe underscores why rigorous safety standards, proper engineering oversight, and worker protections cannot be shortcuts in the name of renewable energy expansion.

The disaster also illustrates why Colorado must resist progressive pressure to abandon proven energy sources in favor of untested, large-scale renewable experiments. Hydropower works—when built and maintained to exacting standards.

Watch whether Colorado environmental groups use this tragedy to push for stricter hydroelectric regulations that could raise energy costs for taxpayers and businesses.
