# Colorado Springs Faces First Legal Challenge Over 2025 Voting Law Enforcement

Colorado Springs is now the test case for whether the state's 2025 voting law will actually stick—and conservatives are watching closely.

The city is being sued for holding municipal elections in April, marking the first enforcement action since the law took effect (Complete Colorado). The lawsuit signals that voting-law compliance is no longer theoretical; it's being litigated in real time across Colorado's largest cities.

Meanwhile, Aurora voters will decide this November whether to shift municipal elections from odd-numbered years to even-numbered years via Ballot Question 3D (Denver Post). The timing matters: moving to even-numbered election years would consolidate local races with state and federal contests, potentially altering turnout patterns and campaign dynamics in Colorado's second-largest city.

**The conservative angle:** These disputes reveal how voting schedules affect electoral outcomes. Odd-year municipal elections traditionally draw smaller, more engaged electorates—often more favorable to fiscally conservative candidates. Shifting to even-numbered years could boost turnout but also invite higher-spending progressive candidates riding national wave elections. Colorado Springs' legal battle will clarify whether the 2025 law's requirements are enforceable statewide.

**Watch:** Whether other Colorado cities challenge the voting law or preemptively move their election cycles before courts force the issue.
